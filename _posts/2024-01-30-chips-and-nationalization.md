*A conversation on a lab outing: chips are infrastructure the way water, steel, and power are. The Big Fund's early mistakes show the market has to drive, but the state won't step away — and the Matthew effect may be doing its work for it.*

On a recent lab outing, chatting with a labmate, we got onto how chips are decent for employment but hard to climb in. In academia, as a PI, it's a landscape of fiefdoms; expensive tape-out costs put independent research out of reach, while strong groups have tape-out resources several times a year, and many groups are forced into researching EDA methodology to pad publications (though other fields seem similar). In industry, a startup can pour hundreds of millions into a tape-out without a splash, and can only lean on big companies or heavy state funding. Build the chip and you still need the software and compiler chain; the foreign examples show that after market competition only a few platform-type companies are left as monopolies. The largest chip companies' controlling relationships are closely tied to the state. Academia's and industry's top layers are closely connected and interact often.

*This is a translation of a piece originally published on Zhihu on January 30, 2024.*

That connects to the recent crashes of the Meituan and Didi platforms, after which some called for nationalizing such livelihood-related infrastructure. Chips — a product at once political, livelihood-related, and frontier technology — are surely important infrastructure too. The trend is that new industries on the infrastructure side will very likely grow closer to the state. Water, steel, power, and nuclear were already in the state's hands; finance and the internet are also consolidating into platforms.

But chip development, especially some early decision mistakes of the IC Big Fund, showed that this still needs the market to drive; the state may have to abandon the whole-nation-mobilization approach (though semiconductor equipment still needs it — that's the main scope of the Big Fund's third phase), with the market as the main driver but the state still participating (the Big Fund, the defense chip business, local government venture funds and policy support — all show the state is still keen to have a hand in). As the source industry of electronics and information (one could say the coming era is compute's), these companies are needed to actively participate in and support important national policies, and to be ready for many possible wartime situations.

Most chip companies' development thinking is toward getting big rather than going deep. Foreign chip history tells us most small companies die and what remains are giants. Among domestic chip companies, the far-ahead one aside, the one most matching the state's expectations is the all-round platform company. Spread out, representative companies are emerging in CPU, GPU, FPGA, DSA, analog. Take the GPU area I know best: several companies seem to think alike — get big, learn from NVIDIA to cover data center, gaming, automotive, taking one hill after another. Beyond the market forcibly created by domestic substitution, AI and automotive are among the few new opportunities. Overall it's still in the first round of survival-of-the-fittest reshuffling; more chip companies are probably closing than starting. Under the current political ideology, perhaps the intent is to let them become a few oligarchs through market competition, and then control them indirectly (equity, market control, administrative control). Perhaps today's increasingly Matthew-effect landscape is exactly in step with the state's nationalizing pace.

What use is this macro view to us individually? Join a big company, and study hard to join the Party. In research, join a big group and hold onto a big thigh. In a word: go with the flow. This piece focuses on digital chips; I don't usually follow process and packaging/test. There are surely many errors of thinking and information; I'm posting to discuss with everyone.
