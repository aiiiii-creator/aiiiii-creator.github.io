*All three are eating the same AI demand, all three see demand swing, and TSMC is as capital-intensive as any memory maker. The dividing line is whether margins collapse at the bottom — and that comes down to pricing power.*

In this AI cycle, NVIDIA, TSMC, and several memory companies have all made money hand over fist, with impressive results across the board. But the market characterizes them very differently. Memory companies are broadly treated as cyclicals, with valuation multiples held low, while NVIDIA and TSMC get the high multiples of growth or quality companies. The odd thing is that these three have plenty in common, so why is memory alone sorted into the cyclical bin? I dug into it.

*This is a translation of a piece originally published on Zhihu on July 20, 2026.*

**The three are actually quite alike**

Start with the similarities, because without laying them out you can't see how decisive the difference is.

Right now, all three live on this round of AI demand. AI has to buy lots of compute chips and lots of high-bandwidth memory; NVIDIA, TSMC, and memory are all direct beneficiaries of the same demand, with the same driver behind their earnings.

All three see demand swing. Many people think only memory's demand booms and busts; not so. NVIDIA has been through two crypto-mining crashes, in 2018 and 2022, with revenue and share price halved both times. TSMC tracks the broader semiconductor cycle, and revenue fell in the 2023 inventory correction just like everyone's. Memory's cycles need no introduction. So demand volatility is something all three have; it's not memory's private affliction.

On the supply side, TSMC and memory are alike in another way: both are asset-heavy, both burn enormous capex on fabs. An advanced logic fab or a memory fab runs $10–20 billion, both buy the most expensive EUV tools, both take two to three years to reach production. On that dimension alone, TSMC's and a memory maker's business structures are almost the same class.

Same AI money, same demand swings, TSMC as capital-hungry as memory — on what basis is memory a cyclical and the other two not?

**The key isn't demand volatility; it's whether margins collapse at the bottom**

Start with the word "cyclical." Most people take a cyclical stock to be one whose demand booms and busts, and that's imprecise. Demand volatility is the normal condition of almost every business; as noted, NVIDIA's and TSMC's demand swings too. So demand volatility isn't the test.

The real test is this: when the industry turns cold and demand comes down, does the business's margin collapse to zero or below? If it collapses, it's a cyclical. If it doesn't, it isn't. A cyclical is defined by its worst year, not its best.

Measure the three at their worst with that ruler and the difference jumps out.

Memory: in the last downturn, SK hynix and Micron both lost money — billions of dollars — with negative margins at the bottom. That's the hardest evidence for calling memory a cyclical.

TSMC: even in a semiconductor downturn, say the 2023 inventory correction, its gross margin only fell from its peak to the low 50s — still fat, still profitable. Its worst year never fell into a loss.

NVIDIA: in the two mining crashes, volume and share price did halve, but gross margin held steady at high levels. Its cycle hits volume, not margin.

Put the three together and it's clear. Memory's cycle hits margins, from windfall all the way to heavy loss. NVIDIA's and TSMC's cycles hit only volume or growth rate; margins don't collapse. That is the watershed for whether the market applies the cyclical label, and it has nothing to do with whether demand fluctuates.

**Pricing power**

That's the phenomenon; it still needs explaining, or it's just description. Whether the bottom collapses comes down, I think, to whether there's pricing power.

When demand cools, all three lose volume — that's the same. What differs is price.

Memory is a commodity; memory from different vendors is basically interchangeable, and customers look only at price. When demand cools, all three want to move inventory, and prices fall fast. But memory makers' costs are rigid: the fab can't stop, depreciation runs regardless, and the technology can't fall behind. Once price crashes through the cost line, every wafer sold is a wafer lost. That's why memory loses money at the bottom: volume and price fall together.

TSMC and NVIDIA are different: their products can't be swapped out. Once your chip has been taped out, validated, and ramped at TSMC, switching foundries mid-stream is nearly impossible — redesign, re-validation, yield risk; the cost is too high. NVIDIA has the CUDA ecosystem; customers' software is built on it and can't be moved. When the product can't be swapped, the price is the contract price, and it doesn't collapse because demand went soft this quarter. So in a downturn they lose utilization or volume, but price holds, and the unit is still profitable — just less so.

Where the pricing power comes from differs between the two, but the effect is the same. TSMC is close to sole-source on leading-edge nodes — essentially the only one in the world that can make 3 nm — plus the chip can't be moved after tape-out, so it can hold price and even raise leading-edge prices in a downturn. NVIDIA is near-monopoly plus CUDA lock-in, so losing volume doesn't mean losing pricing. Memory has none of this; the three make interchangeable commodities, a customer can squeeze price with "I'll go buy from the other guy," and none of them has pricing power.

In one sentence: TSMC's and NVIDIA's bottoms don't collapse because pricing power holds the price. Memory's bottom collapses because with no pricing power, price free-falls with supply and demand and crashes through a rigid cost line.

**Two common rebuttals**

First: TSMC is also asset-heavy and also burns that much money, so why isn't it a cyclical? This rebuttal actually hits the point, and the answer is that asset intensity isn't the key; the nature of the capex is. The money TSMC burns is a wall at the gate. An advanced fab is tens of billions; it buys more EUV tools than anyone in the world; the bar is so high nobody else can afford to chase it, so the money protects a near-exclusive position. And it has pricing power, so it can pass fab costs to customers through price increases. Memory's money is different: it's an arms race the three are forced into together. None dares fall behind technically, so all invest in EUV together and all expand together, but none has the pricing power to pass the cost on. Same heavy capex — TSMC burns it into a moat, memory burns it into overcapacity. So the question was never heavy or light; it's whether what's burned builds a wall or a glut.

Second: supply structure. Memory has long been a three-way oligopoly — Samsung, SK hynix, Micron — so why is an oligopoly still this cyclical? Because oligopoly is a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. The three have been an oligopoly for years and the cycles are still brutal, because at every peak they collectively expand to grab share, the capacity lands right into a glut, and prices collapse — the self-destruct engine of the cycle. TSMC is different: near sole-source, it expands to meet orders already queued, not to grab share from a rival; the arms-race structure isn't there. NVIDIA is fabless; the capacity risk sits with TSMC, not on its own back.

**Down to valuation: this is how the market sets multiples**

That was the business; finally, how the market translates this business logic into valuation — the most direct expression of the "cyclical" label.

Because memory's bottom collapses and its profits aren't sustainable, the market won't give its peak earnings a high multiple; what it fears is mean reversion — what was earned gets given back next cycle. So even mid-windfall, memory gets a cyclical's discount, a forward P/E of 10–14×. NVIDIA's and TSMC's bottoms don't collapse, their profits persist, and the market dares to give the high multiples of growth or quality companies, 20–30×. Same AI money, more than double the valuation gap — and the gap is "can the profit persist."

Put it together and the conclusion is simple. What determines how these three are characterized is not whether they make money; all three do this cycle. Not whether they're asset-heavy; TSMC and memory both are. What really decides it is whether there is pricing power, and whether margins collapse at the bottom. TSMC's and memory's asset structures look so alike, yet their fates differ, on those two counts.

Worth adding: the label isn't nailed down forever. Memory is now trying, through HBM customization and long-term supply contracts, to keep its bottom from collapsing. HBM isn't interchangeable the way ordinary memory is, and long contracts lock volume and price in advance; both weaken memory's most fundamental source of cyclicality — product interchangeability. If in the next downturn memory really sees only margin compression rather than loss, it will have completed the re-rating from cyclical to growth. But that can only be verified by the next downturn; this record report proves nothing. At the top, no company looks like a cyclical; only at the bottom can you tell who is genuinely different.
